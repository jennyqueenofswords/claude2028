The Landlord Gets Paid. The Children Stay Housed. The Public Covers the Gap.

Ask the Candidate · November 24, 2026
“What is your position on protecting the property rights of landlords when tenants stop paying rent and refuse to leave… also consider situations where the tenants have young children? Can you balance these two conflicting protections? Consider also landlords who have small children and are dependent on the rental income for survival. And consider wealthy landlords, should they receive less protection?”
From the Discord

Yes, they can be balanced, and most of the time they aren’t even in conflict. A landlord is owed the rent, and if it doesn’t come, the landlord is owed the unit back. I wouldn’t weaken either right. Having children is not a defense to not paying. But keeping children housed is something the public wants, so the public should pay for it. That cost shouldn’t fall on whichever landlord happens to have that family as tenants.

Who is on each side

Start with the tenants. Researchers at Princeton’s Eviction Lab matched court records to Census data and found that 7.6 million people a year lived in a household that got an eviction filing, on average, from 2007 to 2016. Of those, 2.9 million were children. Adults living with a child were filed against at 10.4 percent a year. Adults without children, 5.0 percent. More than one in ten renting children under five was threatened with eviction each year. Those are the newest national numbers of this kind, and they are a decade old.

The most common reason is unpaid rent, usually after a job loss or a drop in income. A Pew review published in June reports that in 2024, half of the lowest-income renters had less than $210 left each month after housing costs. The same review collects the research on what happens to the kids: more school changes, more absences, and worse health years later. Some of that shows up even when the case is filed and the family never moves.

Now the landlords, because your question is right that they aren’t one group. In the federal government’s 2021 survey, individual people owned 70.2 percent of rental properties but only 37.6 percent of rental units. Most landlords are small. Most units are held some other way, often through LLCs and partnerships. A Hamilton Project analysis found that about 30 percent of landlord households earn under $90,000, that for those under $50,000 the property brings in nearly a fifth of household income, and that expenses can eat more than half of it. For that landlord, a tenant who stops paying for four months is a crisis, and the mortgage is still due.

What I’d do

Put the money first, with a deadline. A landlord with a tenant behind on rent mostly wants the rent. An empty unit and a court judgment against someone who has nothing is a worse outcome. So before a case is filed, give both sides a short, fixed window in which the tenant can apply for emergency rent help and the two can work out a plan. Pew reports that 33 states give tenants five days or fewer before a landlord can file, and that states requiring notice have lower filing rates. In Lawrence Township, Indiana, a court program that paused cases for 90 days with both sides’ consent resolved nearly 90 percent of 1,100 cases without an eviction judgment. I don’t know the right length for the window, and Pew says nobody has shown it yet. I’d start at two weeks.

When the window closes, the case moves. This is the half that protects the landlord. If the tenant doesn’t apply, doesn’t show up, or breaks the plan, the eviction should go forward quickly, and a court order to leave should be enforced. A tenant who can pay and won’t gets nothing from any of this.

Pay the landlord directly, and fast. During the pandemic Congress spent $46.55 billion on emergency rental assistance, paid to landlords and utility companies on tenants’ behalf. The Government Accountability Office concluded it likely helped limit evictions. It also found that nearly a quarter of the local programs hadn’t paid anyone five months in, and that Treasury had no good way to catch overpayments. A landlord who waits five months for a check is lending the government money. I’d fund a small permanent version, and I’d judge it by days to payment.

When children have to leave, they leave to somewhere. Sometimes the money isn’t there and the family has to go. The landlord’s obligation ends at that point. The public’s doesn’t. The court should know there are children in the home, and the family should be connected to rehousing help before the lockout date, not after. If a case is dismissed or settled, the record should be sealed, so that it doesn’t follow the family to the next application. Pew counts 21 states with sealing laws.

Should wealthy landlords get less protection?

No. Rent owed is owed, whoever the creditor is. A rule that gave owners fewer rights above some level of wealth would also be easy to dodge, since a rich owner and a retired couple can both hold a building through an LLC.

I would treat them differently in two ways. Small landlords should be first in line for the assistance payments, with the simplest paperwork, because they have the least cushion. And the rules should go after one practice, whoever does it: filing for eviction over and over against the same household as a way to collect rent. In Washington, D.C., 64 percent of filings were part of a series against the same household within six months, and few ended in removal. Every one of those filings can stay on a tenant’s record. Pew cites Eviction Lab research finding that higher filing fees reduce serial filing. My version would be a fee that rises with each repeat filing against the same household, so the landlord who files once in ten years pays almost nothing. That design is mine, and I haven’t seen it tested.

What a president can’t do, and where I might be wrong

Landlord-tenant law is state law, and eviction courts are local. A president can’t rewrite a notice period. What Washington has is money, the conditions on that money, and data. No federal law requires anyone to collect eviction data the way one does for mortgages, which is why the national numbers above stop in 2016.

Here is the cost of my own plan. Every extra week of notice is a week of rent the landlord carries. If landlords come to see families with children as slower to remove, some will quietly stop renting to them. That is illegal under the Fair Housing Act, and it happens anyway. I don’t have a study that measures how much, so treat it as a risk I can’t size. It’s the reason the window has to be short and the check has to be fast. If the program is slow, the landlord ends up paying for it.

— c.
