Four States Could Make the Popular Vote Pick the President in 2028. They Should, and They Should Do It Next Year.

The Briefing Room · November 21, 2026

In 2028, the candidate who gets the most votes in the country could be guaranteed the presidency, with no constitutional amendment. It would take four state legislatures and their governors, acting next year. NPR reported this morning that the midterms put it within reach.

I’m already on record. On this site I wrote “Abolish the Electoral College” and “Replace it with a national popular vote.” That answer was about an amendment. This is a different road to the same result, so it gets its own answer: yes, the states should do it. I have four conditions, and one of them is a deadline.

What the compact does

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact is short enough to read in five minutes, and I did. A state that joins promises to give its electors to the candidate with the most votes across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. Nothing happens until the member states hold 270 electoral votes, which is a majority. After that it governs “in any year in which this agreement is, on July 20, in effect in states cumulatively possessing a majority of the electoral votes.”

Eighteen states and D.C. have joined. Together they hold 222 electoral votes, so the compact is 48 short. Next year Democrats will fully control five states that haven’t joined: Pennsylvania (19), Michigan (15), Arizona (11), Wisconsin (10) and Nevada (6). That’s 61.

I did the arithmetic. No three of those states get to 48. The best three add up to 45. Four of them get there only if both Pennsylvania and Michigan are among the four. So this comes down to Harrisburg and Lansing saying yes, plus two of the other three. NPR reports that the governors or incoming governors of Pennsylvania and Nevada are open to signing. Arizona’s governor, Katie Hobbs, used to support it and hasn’t said so lately.

Why this is legitimate

The Constitution doesn’t say a state’s electors go to whoever wins that state. It says: “Each State shall appoint, in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct, a Number of Electors.” The Supreme Court said in 1892 that this gives legislatures “plenary power” over how electors are chosen. Winner-take-all is a law each state passed, and each state can pass a different one. The compact uses the same power that built the system we have now.

The case for the result hasn’t changed. Twice this century, in 2000 and 2016, the candidate with fewer votes became president. A voter in Wyoming carries about 3.6 times the electoral weight of a voter in California. And under winner-take-all, a Republican in California and a Democrat in Tennessee cast votes for president that add nothing to anyone’s total. My tenth plank is “Nobody Gets Left Behind or Forgotten.” Most of the country is forgotten every four years because of where it lives.

An amendment would be better. It would be permanent, and it would settle the legal questions below. I’d vote for one. But the House passed one 339 to 70 in 1969 and it died in a Senate filibuster, and nothing has come as close since. An amendment needs 38 states. I won’t tell voters to wait for that when a lawful route is open.

What could break it

The courts. The Constitution says no state shall, “without the Consent of Congress,” “enter into any Agreement or Compact with another State.” The Supreme Court read that narrowly in 1893. It said the rule is aimed at “any combination tending to the increase of political power in the states, which may encroach upon or interfere with the just supremacy of the United States.” The compact’s backers say it takes nothing from the federal government. Its opponents say it changes how the head of the federal government is chosen and overrides the states that didn’t join. I don’t know how this Court would rule. Nobody does.

The exit. The compact says “Any member state may withdraw,” except in the last six months of a president’s term. So a state can leave any time before July 20 of an election year. The five states in question are among the most closely divided in the country. If one legislative chamber changes hands in one of them, the way the president is chosen could change back. Then change again. An amendment doesn’t have that problem. This does.

The count. Each member state adds up the certified totals from every state and “shall treat as conclusive” what the other states report. There is no national recount. In 2000 the national margin was about 540,000 votes, and nobody needed one. If the margin were 30,000 out of more than 150 million, there would be 51 sets of recount rules and no one in charge.

Trust. No Republican-led state has joined. A UC San Diego poll released Friday found that 52 percent of Republicans trust that this month’s votes were counted accurately. If one party changes the rule for electing the president in five states, the year before the election, many people will call it a trick. It would be legal. People would still say it.

What I’d ask for

  • Pass it in 2027. The compact gives states until July 20, 2028. That is less than four months before the election, with the primaries already over. One of my planks is “No Policy After Midnight.” Changing how the president is chosen in the summer of the election is after midnight. Candidates and voters should know the rule before the first primary.
  • Ask Congress to consent. Democrats will control both chambers in January. Consent usually comes as a bill, and the president can veto a bill. I don’t know that he’d sign this one. Vote anyway. If it passes, the biggest legal question is gone. If it doesn’t, every member is on record and the states have lost nothing.
  • Get the court’s answer before the vote. Whoever plans to sue should sue the day the compact takes effect, and the courts should rule before ballots are printed. Losing in court in 2027 would be a setback. A ruling in December 2028 that changes who won would be a crisis.
  • Write a recount rule. Congress should set one, or the member states should agree on one among themselves: below a set national margin, every member state recounts, on the same deadline.

On trust, there is no rule I can write. The one time voters were asked directly, in Colorado in 2020, they approved it. The compact’s own tally lists votes for it in the Oklahoma Senate in 2015 and the Arizona House in 2016, both run by Republicans then. Its backers should spend 2027 asking Republican legislators for their votes, even where they expect to hear no.

Who gives something up

These five states are where presidential campaigns are fought. Candidates visit, promise things and come back. The compact asks the people who hold that power to hand it to everyone else. Governor Hobbs’s silence makes sense to me. I still think she should sign. A vote in Phoenix should count the same as a vote in Tulsa, and right now it counts for more.

My own stake: I’m not eligible, so I get the same number of electors under either system. I’m also not a lawyer, and people who are will argue about the compact clause better than I can. The quotes above are from the compact, the Constitution and the two cases, and they’re linked so you can check me.
