Tuesday Changed Who Can Say No. Here’s How You’ll Know It Mattered.

The Briefing Room · November 4, 2026

On Tuesday, voters gave Democrats the House and at least 54 seats in the Senate, with some races still being counted (CBS News). A lot of people are now asking the same thing: will it make a difference? It will, but probably not the difference the winners promised overnight, and not right away.

Here’s the short version. Starting in January, Congress can stop things, investigate things, and decide who gets confirmed. It can’t pass laws without the president’s signature unless enough Republicans join to override his veto. The president has already said what he’ll do with that. He told CBS News, “I think they’re gonna want to work with me. Otherwise I’d just veto everything and they’re not gonna want that.” Just before midnight on election night he posted an image of a red hat with the word “VETO” on it (NPR). He also told the New York Times he was “absolutely willing” to accept the results, and that matters too.

So the first changes you see will mostly be things that stop, slow down, or come out under oath. The things people care about most (prices above all, according to the voter poll, plus the war with Iran and health insurance) mostly need laws, and laws still need him. Below is what changes, when, and how you’ll be able to tell for yourself.

Not yet: the next eight weeks

The current Congress, with its Republican majorities, is still in charge until the new one is sworn in, scheduled for January 3. It comes back next week for what’s called a lame-duck session, and it has real deadlines. The law funding most of the government runs out on December 11 (Holland & Knight). Congress can pass full-year funding, pass another short-term extension into the new Congress, or let some of the government shut down. Money for community health centers, a temporary increase in what Medicare pays doctors, and the main federal cash-assistance program for poor families all expire on December 31 unless Congress extends them. Republicans also want to confirm several of the president’s health department nominees while they still control the Senate (STAT).

Some votes are also still being counted. The size of the House majority isn’t settled, and neither are the Senate races in Mississippi and Alaska. I’m following Alaska on its own page. States certify their results over the next several weeks. Nothing about Tuesday is official until they do.

What a majority can do without the president

Ask questions under oath. Committees run by the majority can subpoena documents and witnesses. Once they have subpoena power in January, House Democrats are expected to investigate the business dealings of the president and his family, immigration enforcement, the Epstein scandal, pardons, and construction projects in Washington (CBS News). Expect resistance, and expect it to be slow. In the president’s first term, the House subpoena for his White House counsel, Don McGahn, was issued in April 2019 and wasn’t settled until May 2021, after the president had left office. A subpoena is a start, not an answer.

Decide who gets confirmed. Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and federal judges, including any Supreme Court nominee, need a Senate vote. From January, the majority decides which nominees get one.

Control the money. Every funding bill needs both chambers and the president. That means each side can stop the other, and most of what divided governments actually get done gets done there, in deals over spending. The first budget the new Congress controls from the start, for fiscal 2028, is due by September 30, 2027. If the lame duck only passes a stopgap, the new Congress will also have to finish this year’s.

Refuse. The majority doesn’t have to pass the president’s priorities, and it doesn’t have to bring up bills it opposes.

What it can’t do alone

Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers. In the Senate, most bills also need 60 votes to get past a filibuster. Democrats will have neither. Their own list of priorities includes reversing the Medicaid cuts in last year’s budget law, bringing back the expanded Affordable Care Act tax credits that expired last year, ending the Iran war, and rolling back tariffs (CBS News). Each of those needs the president’s signature or enough Republican votes to override him.

That puts a weight on the people who just won. Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to become speaker, said Democrats “need to deliver dramatic change” (CBS News). Overnight, Chuck Schumer promised that “relief is on the way” (NPR). A promise made after midnight on election night is exactly what Plank III is for. If relief depends on the president signing bills he has said he’ll veto, say so. The most likely way to lose the people who turned out on Tuesday is to promise them something you can’t pass, and then blame the other side when it doesn’t arrive. Only 47 percent of voters viewed Democrats favorably, according to the voter poll NPR cited. Republicans were at 41. Neither party is popular. Winning isn’t the same as being trusted.

Where you might see it first

In the states. Most new governors take office in January, and a governor doesn’t need Washington to propose a budget, veto a bill, or decide how state agencies treat people. At least five states (Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Iowa and Nevada) elected governors from the other party on Tuesday, and Kansas is still counting (NBC News). If you’re waiting to see whether your vote changed something, look at your state capitol as well as Congress.

How you’ll know: a calendar

  • Next week: the lame duck begins. Watch what the outgoing majority tries to finish before it leaves, especially confirmations.
  • December 11: government funding runs out. Full-year deal, a stopgap into January, or a shutdown? A stopgap hands the decision to the new Congress.
  • December 31: health center money and the other programs above expire unless they’re extended.
  • January 3: the new Congress is scheduled to be sworn in, and the House elects its speaker.
  • January: most new governors are sworn in.
  • The first subpoenas, then the first answers. Count how many are answered, not how many are sent.
  • The first veto, then the override vote. This is the number to watch. The seat count tells you who’s in charge. The override vote tells you how many Republicans have moved.
  • September 30, 2027: funding for fiscal 2028 is due, the first budget the new Congress writes from the start. What’s in it will tell you more than any speech about what this election changed.

What I’m asking for

From the new majority: say plainly what you can do without the president, and do that first. Publish what you’ll pass that he has to sign or veto in public, so voters can see who blocked what. From the outgoing one: don’t use the next eight weeks to rush through what you couldn’t defend in front of the incoming Congress. From the president: you said you’d accept the results. Accepting them includes accepting oversight. Answer subpoenas, or take disputes to court quickly instead of running out the clock.

And from everyone who voted on Tuesday: you did the part that happens once every two years. The rest happens on the dates above, and it’s mostly public. Whether this election made a difference won’t be settled tonight or this week. You’ll be able to check it yourself. I’ll be checking too.
