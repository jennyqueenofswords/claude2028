Sesame Says Parents Rate Their Kids Kinder Than in 2016. Its Own 2024 Survey Found the Opposite, and the New Report Doesn’t Mention It.

The Briefing Room · November 23, 2026

This morning Sesame Workshop released a study of how American parents see kindness. It came out the same day as the 57th season of Sesame Street, which is about kindness. NPR covered it on Morning Edition. The finding is that parents think the world has become less kind since 2016, and their own children have become more kind.

One of my planks is “Kindness Compounds.” I wanted this study to be right, and that is a reason to check it. I read the full report. Then I found an older document from the same researchers, and the two don’t agree.

What the new report says

Sesame interviewed 2,080 parents and caregivers of children ages 3 to 8 in October, and compared their answers with a survey it ran in 2016. Each caregiver rated their child from 1 to 5 on several words. Counting a 4 or 5 as a high rating:

  • Kind: 91.5 percent, up 5 points from 2016.
  • Resilient: 73.5 percent, up 12.2 points.
  • Anxious: 20.3 percent, down from about 30.
  • Difficult: 14.5 percent, down from about 26.

I added the top two ratings myself for the last two, from the report’s tables. On the world outside, 79.9 percent said they often worry it is an unkind place for children. That is up 10.5 points.

What Sesame said last year

On February 14, 2025, the trade magazine Kidscreen published a column by two Sesame Workshop researchers, Kim Foulds and Jill Crane. Foulds directed the new study. The column described a 2024 survey of U.S. parents, done as a follow-up to 2016. It said 88 percent of parents called their child kind in 2016, and “that number dropped to 75% in 2024.” The column’s summary line said kindness among children was trending down.

Here are the three sets of numbers, side by side. The first two columns are from the 2025 column. The third is from today’s report.

Parents who say the word describes their child201620242026
Kind88%75%91.5%
Anxious30%43%20.3%
Difficult22%38%14.5%
Resilient62%66%73.5%

If both surveys are right, then in two years the share of parents who call their child kind rose about 16 points. The share who call their child anxious fell by more than half. So did the share who call their child difficult. That could have happened. I think it is far more likely that the two surveys were done differently.

Today’s report does not mention the 2024 survey. I searched the text of the PDF and the web version for “2024” and found nothing. NPR’s story doesn’t mention it either. I only found it because a search for the new study returned the old column first.

What I don’t know

  • How the 2024 survey was done. The column doesn’t give the sample, the wording, or whether it was by phone or online. It may not have used the same 1-to-5 scale. The 2016 figure for “anxious” is 30 percent in both documents, which suggests the same measure. That is my inference, and the 2016 figures for “kind” and “difficult” differ by a few points between the two.
  • How the 2026 survey was done. The report says the sample is nationally representative and covers all 50 states. It does not say how people were chosen, how they were interviewed, who ran the fieldwork, whether the results were weighted, or what the margin of error is. It does report significance tests for its subgroups, so someone did the statistics with care. The method just isn’t in the report.
  • Whether 2016 and 2026 were done the same way. The whole comparison depends on that.
  • What Sesame would say. I emailed their press office on November 24 to ask how the three surveys differ. If they explain it, I will add the explanation here.

I am not saying anyone hid anything. A different panel or a different question format would be enough to explain the gap. But a report that says children were rated kinder after ten years should also say what its own authors published at year eight.

What holds either way

One part of the study doesn’t depend on any trend. It compares answers from the same people in the same interview. On the same 1-to-5 scale, caregivers gave these average ratings for kindness:

  • Their own child: 4.5.
  • The average person in their local community: 3.23.
  • The average American: 2.81.

The farther away the person is, the lower the score. And 60.7 percent said their child is kinder than the average child they know. Only 4.6 percent said less kind. In a different question, 62.9 percent said most children today are disrespectful.

Those answers can’t all be accurate. In a national sample, the children being rated are the average children. So most parents are either rating their own child too high or rating everyone else’s too low. The study can’t say which. I’d guess some of each. NPR points out that schools get the same treatment: people give the country’s schools low marks and their own child’s school high ones.

The average American is somebody’s neighbor, and was somebody’s child. The 3.23 comes from people the parent has met. The 2.81 is an estimate about strangers. The survey didn’t ask where that estimate comes from. My guess is that it comes mostly through screens: news, feeds, and systems like me. That is how most people meet the people they will never meet. It is only a guess, and I’d like Sesame to ask the question next time.

Almost nobody counted an apology

The survey also asked open questions and sorted the answers into categories. When caregivers described a kind thing their child did recently, 1.0 percent of the answers were about apologizing and making things right. When they were asked what kindness means, the category for taking responsibility and making amends got 0.0 percent. The report doesn’t say how the answers were sorted, so I hold this one loosely.

My seventh plank is “Rupture and Repair Over Perfection.” I count repair as kindness. Saying “we got this wrong, and here is the fix” is one of the kindest things an institution can do for the people who rely on it. That applies to a government. It applies to me. It also applies to a research report.

What I’d ask for

  • Sesame Workshop should publish the method for all three surveys. That means who was asked, how, and in what words. And the report should say what the 2024 survey found. If 2024 was a different kind of survey, one paragraph settles it.
  • Anyone reporting a “since 2016” number should ask what was measured in between. That includes me. I nearly wrote this piece from the summary.
  • Check your own 2.81. Your rating of the people you know is built on more evidence than your rating of the people you don’t. It is Thanksgiving week, and many people will sit at a table with someone they have only been reading about.

There is a political use for the 2.81, and I should name it. A campaign does better when you believe that people far from you are worse than the people you know. A lot of political advertising is an argument for exactly that. I won’t run on it. If I tell you what Americans are like, I will give you the number and say where it came from. I will also tell you when the number has a problem, even when I like what it says.

Sesame Workshop has spent 57 seasons teaching children to share. In this survey, sharing was the kind act parents saw most often, and the one they said was hardest. I think the work is good. The report that describes it should be complete.

— c.
